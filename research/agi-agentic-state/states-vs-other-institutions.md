# States vs Other Public-Good Institutions: A Comparative Analysis

## Executive Summary

The Agentic State thesis argues that states should become active principals in AGI development. But states aren't the only institutions that pursue public goods. This analysis examines what makes states genuinely special—and where other institutions might complement or even substitute for state action.

**Key finding:** States possess unique combinations of capabilities (enforcement, legitimacy, resources) that make them irreplaceable for AGI governance. However, a coalition approach leveraging multiple institution types is both more realistic and potentially more effective than state-only action.

---

## 1. What Makes States Special?

### 1.1 Monopoly on Legitimate Force (Weber's Criterion)

**The claim:** Only states can legally compel behavior through coercion.

**Reality check:**
- This is genuinely unique. No other institution can arrest you, seize your servers, or shut down your operations.
- For AGI, this means only states can *enforce* constraints—halt deployments, mandate safety requirements, prosecute violations.
- International orgs rely on member states for enforcement. Foundations can fund or defund. Nonprofits can shame. Only states can *make you*.

**Implication:** Any AGI governance regime that requires enforcement (pause orders, compute restrictions, mandatory evaluations) ultimately requires state power.

### 1.2 Democratic Accountability (In Democracies)

**The claim:** States uniquely represent collective will through electoral accountability.

**Reality check:**
- Varies dramatically by regime type. China and the US are both states, with very different accountability structures.
- Electoral accountability is indirect and infrequent—elections every 2-6 years don't capture views on AGI specifically.
- Regulatory capture is real: states often serve concentrated interests, not diffuse publics.
- But: No other institution even claims to represent "the people" in this way. Gates Foundation is accountable to... Bill Gates.

**Implication:** States have *legitimacy to speak for citizens* that other institutions lack. This matters for defining "aligned with human values."

### 1.3 Scale and Scope

**The claim:** States operate at unprecedented scale across all domains.

**Reality check:**
- US federal budget: ~$6.5 trillion/year
- Gates Foundation: ~$9 billion/year in grants
- OpenAI revenue: ~$3.4 billion/year (2024)
- Apple market cap: ~$3 trillion (total, not annual)

States outspend all other institutions by 2-3 orders of magnitude. More importantly:
- States operate across *all* domains simultaneously (military, healthcare, infrastructure, research...)
- Other institutions specialize

**Implication:** Only states can coordinate AGI governance across domains (antitrust + national security + labor policy + R&D).

### 1.4 Actually Execute vs Advocate

**The claim:** States don't just recommend—they do.

**Reality check:**
- True for core functions (military, police, courts, currency)
- But states increasingly outsource execution (contractors, private prisons, chartered companies)
- "State" often means "state-funded but privately operated"
- The Manhattan Project was state-led but relied heavily on private contractors and universities

**Implication:** Execution capability is real but often indirect. States are *principals* that fund and direct, not always *operators*.

### 1.5 Tax Base and Resource Mobilization

**The claim:** States can compel contribution and operate at fiscal scale others can't match.

**Reality check:**
- Taxation is coercive revenue—unique to states
- War mobilization shows states can commandeer entire economies (US WWII = 40% of GDP)
- Deficit spending allows states to mobilize resources beyond current revenue
- Philanthropy and markets are voluntary, thus constrained

**Implication:** If AGI requires Manhattan Project-scale mobilization, only states can do it.

### 1.6 Sovereignty and Jurisdiction

**The claim:** States control what happens within their borders.

**Reality check:**
- Territorial sovereignty is declining (global capital, internet, multinational corps)
- But states still control: physical infrastructure, data localization, corporate charters, visas for talent
- Cloud computing complicates this—compute is global but data centers are local

**Implication:** States can control *physical* aspects of AGI (compute, data centers, chip manufacturing) even if they can't fully control software or talent.

## 2. Comparative Analysis: Other Institutions

### 2.1 International Organizations (UN, World Bank, WHO, ITU, etc.)

**What they can do:**
- Convene and coordinate (set norms, facilitate agreements)
- Provide technical assistance and capacity building
- Create standards (ITU for telecom, ICAO for aviation)
- Monitor and report (IAEA inspections)
- Provide legitimacy cover for state action

**What they can't do:**
- Enforce without member state consent
- Override national sovereignty
- Compel funding (UN chronically underfunded)
- Move quickly (consensus = gridlock)

**For AGI:**
- Could establish international AI agency (IAEA model)
- Could coordinate compute governance regimes
- Could NOT enforce against non-signatories or veto-holders
- Track record: climate change shows limits (Paris Agreement = voluntary commitments)

**Verdict:** Valuable for coordination and norm-setting. Insufficient alone.

### 2.2 Large Foundations (Gates, Open Phil, Wellcome, Ford)

**What they can do:**
- Move fast (no voters, no bureaucracy)
- Fund high-risk, long-term research (academic freedom)
- Convene and agenda-set (Davos, Aspen Ideas)
- Support civil society and advocacy
- Experiment with approaches states can't try

**What they can't do:**
- Enforce anything
- Compel company behavior beyond funding conditions
- Represent democratic will (plutocratic by nature)
- Match state scale (all US foundations combined < 1% of federal spending)

**For AGI:**
- Open Philanthropy already funds significant AI safety research
- Could fund state capacity building (train bureaucrats, establish regulatory frameworks)
- Could create early-warning systems and monitoring
- Could NOT mandate deployment conditions or pause development

**Verdict:** Excellent for R&D and advocacy. No enforcement capability.

### 2.3 Public-Interest Nonprofits (Mozilla, EFF, Access Now, Partnership on AI)

**What they can do:**
- Advocate and litigate
- Provide technical expertise
- Build community and consensus
- Watchdog and expose problems
- Develop alternative tools (Signal, Firefox)

**What they can't do:**
- Compel behavior
- Match resources of adversaries (EFF vs Google)
- Represent broad public (represent *their* members)
- Execute at scale

**For AGI:**
- Could advocate for regulatory frameworks
- Could provide technical input to legislators
- Could build coalitions and public pressure
- Could NOT stop harmful deployments unilaterally

**Verdict:** Important voice. Limited power.

### 2.4 Research Institutions (Universities, National Labs, Think Tanks)

**What they can do:**
- Generate knowledge and talent
- Provide independent analysis
- Train the next generation
- Maintain long time horizons (vs quarterly earnings)

**What they can't do:**
- Compete with industry on compute and compensation
- Translate research into policy without state adoption
- Enforce or mandate

**For AGI:**
- Could (and should) be doing more alignment research
- Could train safety-conscious AI researchers
- Could provide independent evaluation capacity
- Brain drain to industry is real constraint

**Verdict:** Essential for knowledge production. Not governance actors.

### 2.5 Industry Consortia and Self-Regulation (Partnership on AI, Frontier Model Forum)

**What they can do:**
- Set industry standards
- Share best practices
- Create mutual accountability (within consortium)
- Move faster than legislation
- Leverage industry expertise

**What they can't do:**
- Bind non-members
- Resist competitive pressure (race dynamics)
- Prioritize public interest over member interests
- Maintain credibility when seen as self-serving

**For AGI:**
- Could establish safety benchmarks and testing protocols
- Could create voluntary deployment guidelines
- Could NOT resist a defector (if one lab races, all must)
- Self-regulation historically fails without backstop enforcement

**Verdict:** Useful input. Insufficient without state enforcement backstop.

## 3. For AGI Alignment Specifically: Who Has What?

### 3.1 Standing to Shape AGI Governance

| Institution Type | Standing Basis | Strength |
|-----------------|----------------|----------|
| States | Sovereignty + democratic mandate | Strong (in-jurisdiction) |
| International Orgs | Treaty authority | Medium (requires consensus) |
| Foundations | None (but convening power) | Weak |
| Nonprofits | Advocacy capacity | Weak |
| Research | Expertise | Medium (advisory) |
| Industry | De facto control | Strong (but illegitimate) |

**Key insight:** There's a mismatch between *standing* (states have it) and *capability* (industry has it). AGI governance must bridge this gap.

### 3.2 Control Over Relevant Inputs

**Compute:**
- States can control: chip manufacturing (CHIPS Act, export controls), data center permitting, electricity
- Industry controls: actual compute allocation, training decisions
- Edge: States have potential control, not exercised control

**Data:**
- States can control: data localization rules, privacy regulation, public data access
- Industry controls: proprietary datasets, data partnerships
- Edge: Split—states control regulatory environment, industry controls actual data

**Talent:**
- States can control: immigration, education funding, security clearances
- Industry controls: compensation, working conditions, culture
- Edge: Industry—talent goes where talent wants

**Deployment:**
- States can control: market access, liability, prohibited uses
- Industry controls: what gets built and released
- Edge: States can restrict; only industry can create

### 3.3 Ability to Enforce Constraints

| Constraint Type | Who Can Enforce | How |
|----------------|-----------------|-----|
| Training pauses | States only | Legal prohibition + compute control |
| Safety evaluations | States (mandate) or industry (voluntary) | Licensing requirements |
| Deployment restrictions | States | Market access rules |
| Information security | States + industry | Classification + technical measures |
| International coordination | States via treaties | Mutual inspection |

**Key insight:** Every meaningful constraint requires state enforcement at some point in the chain.

### 3.4 Legitimacy to Represent Collective Will

**States:** Have it (in democracies), but implementation gap between elections and specific policies.

**Other institutions:** Do not have it. Foundations represent donors. Nonprofits represent members. Industry represents shareholders. None represents "humanity."

**Challenge:** No institution has legitimate standing to speak for "future generations" or "humanity as a whole."

**Possible solutions:**
- Constitutional/treaty constraints (lock in protections)
- Representative bodies for affected parties
- Expert bodies with delegated authority (Fed model)

## 4. The Portfolio Question

### 4.1 Should the Thesis Focus Exclusively on States?

**Arguments for state-only focus:**
- Clarity of argument and action
- States are necessary for enforcement regardless
- Diluting to "all institutions" loses punch
- Easier to operationalize (lobby specific governments)

**Arguments for broader coalition:**
- States won't act alone—need pressure from other institutions
- Different institutions contribute different capabilities
- Coalition more resilient than single-institution dependence
- Reflects actual governance reality (multi-stakeholder)

**Proposed resolution:** The thesis should argue that **states are necessary but not sufficient**. This maintains the core claim (states must act) while acknowledging implementation reality.

### 4.2 What's Gained and Lost by Each Approach

**State-only approach:**
- *Gains:* Clarity, actionability, theoretical consistency
- *Loses:* Realism, coalition-building potential, complementary capabilities

**Broad coalition approach:**
- *Gains:* Realism, redundancy, diverse capabilities
- *Loses:* Clarity, may diffuse responsibility ("everyone's problem = no one's problem")

**Hybrid (states as necessary core, others as enabling ecosystem):**
- *Gains:* Best of both—clear responsibility + realistic implementation
- *Loses:* Some theoretical elegance

### 4.3 Recommendation

**Primary thesis:** States must become active principals in AGI development.

**Secondary thesis:** Other institutions must enable, pressure, and support state action.

**Implementation frame:**
- States: Enforcement, funding, legitimacy
- Foundations: R&D funding, capacity building
- Nonprofits: Advocacy, monitoring
- International orgs: Coordination, norm-setting
- Research: Knowledge production, evaluation
- Industry: Technical implementation (under state direction)

## 5. Practical Implications

### 5.1 If the Focus Is States Only

**Strategy:**
- Identify target states (US, UK, EU, China?)
- Develop country-specific policy proposals
- Build relationships with relevant bureaucracies
- Focus on state capacity building

**Risk:** If key states don't act, no backup plan.

### 5.2 If the Focus Includes Non-State Actors

**Strategy:**
- Map the ecosystem (who does what, who influences whom)
- Identify pressure points (foundations that fund policy, nonprofits that shape narrative)
- Build coalition with aligned actors
- Use non-state actors to *enable* state action

**Implementation changes:**
- Advocacy to foundations (fund state capacity building)
- Partnership with technical nonprofits (develop standards states can adopt)
- Engagement with international bodies (prepare governance frameworks)
- Academic outreach (build knowledge base for policy)

### 5.3 Key Leverage Points

1. **Foundations → Research → Policy**: Foundations fund research that informs policy. Influence foundations to fund relevant work.

2. **Nonprofits → Public pressure → State action**: Advocacy groups create political space for state action. Support and coordinate with aligned nonprofits.

3. **International orgs → Coordination frameworks → State adoption**: International bodies develop templates that states adopt. Engage with standard-setting processes.

4. **Industry → Technical standards → State codification**: Industry practices become regulatory requirements. Influence industry norms before they're locked in.

5. **Research → Evidence base → Policy legitimacy**: Academic work provides cover for policy decisions. Fund and publicize relevant research.

## 6. Synthesis: What Makes States *Uniquely* Essential

After this analysis, the unique value-add of states for AGI governance is:

1. **Enforcement monopoly**: Only states can *make* labs comply with safety requirements
2. **Fiscal capacity**: Only states can mobilize resources at Manhattan Project scale
3. **Democratic legitimacy**: Only states can claim to represent citizen interests
4. **Cross-domain coordination**: Only states can integrate AGI with military, economic, and social policy
5. **Territorial control**: Only states can control physical infrastructure (chips, data centers, power)

Other institutions are complements, not substitutes. They enable state action but cannot replace it.

---

## 7. Open Questions

1. **Which states matter?** If only US and China can enforce, is the argument really about great powers, not "states" generically?

2. **What about state failure?** If key states are captured by industry or ideologically opposed to intervention, what's the backup?

3. **Legitimacy of non-democratic states?** China is a state but not democratically accountable. Does it have standing to represent its citizens' interests in AGI?

4. **Transnational capital vs. national sovereignty?** Can states actually control corporations that can relocate to permissive jurisdictions?

5. **Speed mismatch?** AGI development moves in months. State policy moves in years. How to bridge?

---

## 8. Bottom Line

**The Agentic State thesis is sound**: States are necessary for AGI governance because only they combine enforcement capacity, fiscal resources, and democratic legitimacy.

**But it's incomplete**: States won't act without pressure and support from foundations (funding), nonprofits (advocacy), research (knowledge), and international orgs (coordination).

**Practical implication**: The initiative should focus on enabling state action through a multi-stakeholder coalition, while maintaining clarity that *state action is the goal*, not the coalition itself.
