# Layer Analysis 7-12: Public Interest Framing Assessment

## Overview

This analysis applies the public interest rubric to Enablement Layers 7-12 of the Agentic State Vision Paper. The core question guiding this analysis:

> "In an era of many AGIs, how do we ensure they serve the public interest?"

Audience: Democratic political decision-makers (parliament, PM, ministers)

---

## Layer 7: Agent Governance: Accountability, Safety, and Redress

### Current Framing

This chapter argues that agentic government systems require fundamentally new accountability mechanisms because traditional bureaucratic oversight breaks down when:
- Speed mismatches occur (systems process thousands while humans review weekly)
- Attribution becomes complex (decisions emerge from multiple AI systems, not identifiable humans)
- Cross-organisational coordination creates accountability gaps

The chapter proposes an interlocking framework: ethical (rights-preserving architecture), legal (delegation/attribution/liability), appeal (citizen control and redress), and institutional evolution for oversight bodies.

**Key quote:** "Citizens and companies (rightly) expect a higher degree of due process, accountability and redress from governments than the private sector."

### Public Interest Alignment

**Assessment: Strong**

This is one of the most strongly public-interest-aligned chapters. It explicitly centers on citizens' rights and positions accountability as non-negotiable:

- "Fundamental ethical principles — fair treatment, transparency, and respect for human dignity — must be embedded directly into agentic system architecture"
- Procedural fairness, equal treatment, and transparency are framed as **design requirements**, not afterthoughts
- Citizens positioned as rights-holders who can inspect, challenge, and override system decisions

The chapter explicitly argues that agentic systems could *surpass* current bureaucratic accountability: "Done right, they can even surpass the status quo — embedding fairness, transparency, and redress directly into technical architecture."

### Accountability & Democratic Oversight

**Assessment: Present**

Multiple mechanisms explicitly addressed:

1. **Rollback and Preview Windows:** "Before consequential actions, systems must provide preview periods allowing review and contestation"
2. **Human Override:** "Citizens and businesses retain rights to human review of consequential decisions"
3. **Layered Transparency:** Explanations tailored to citizens, experts, and auditors
4. **Bring Your Own Oversight:** "Watchdogs, NGOs, and individuals should be able to deploy independent oversight agents to interrogate decision logs, test fairness, and flag anomalies"

This last point is particularly strong — it positions civil society as active participants in accountability, not just beneficiaries.

### Outside-In Pressures

**Assessment: Addressed**

The chapter explicitly acknowledges external pressures through the "Bring Your Own Oversight" concept:

> "Oversight should be decentralised, not monopolised by state institutions. Just as citizens may 'bring their own agent' to access services, watchdogs, NGOs, and individuals should be able to deploy independent oversight agents..."

This recognizes that government isn't the only actor ensuring public interest — civil society, independent watchdogs, and citizens themselves are part of the accountability ecosystem.

### Multi-Vector Theory of Change

**Assessment: Acknowledged**

The chapter explicitly embraces multiple vectors:
- Government sets technical architecture and legal frameworks
- Civil society deploys independent oversight agents
- Citizens exercise individual rights to review and override
- Independent audit bodies certify compliance

**Quote:** "Standardised interfaces and open data streams make this possible, embedding pluralism directly into system governance and ensuring accountability is continuously contested and verifiable."

### Gap Assessment

**🟢 GREEN**

This layer already fits the public interest framing exceptionally well. It doesn't merely assume government serves the public interest — it creates mechanisms to *ensure* it does through:
- Rights embedded in architecture
- Citizen redress at machine speed
- Pluralistic oversight (government + civil society + individuals)
- Clear attribution and liability chains

### Specific Recommendations

Minor additions to strengthen further:

1. **Explicit democratic mandate framing:** Add language connecting these mechanisms to democratic legitimacy (parliament sets the boundaries, citizens hold ultimate authority)

2. **Failure scenarios:** What happens when these mechanisms fail? Add examples of accountability breakdown and recovery

3. **AGI-specific considerations:** Current text focuses on narrow AI agents. As systems approach AGI, the stakes intensify — worth a paragraph on how these mechanisms scale to more capable systems

4. **International dimension:** Cross-border accountability when agents operate transnationally

## Layer 8: Data and Privacy

### Current Framing

The chapter argues that agentic AI has fundamentally different data needs than traditional computing — agents need data environments encoding "context, relationships, permissions, history, uncertainty, and meaning." It then positions government as "society's data steward" and proposes three mindset shifts:
- Data = Infrastructure
- Data = Decisions  
- Data Skills = Basic Competence

The chapter covers the full data lifecycle and positions "open by default, closed by exception" as the guiding principle.

### Public Interest Alignment

**Assessment: Moderate**

The chapter has strong public interest elements but also efficiency-focused framing that sometimes dominates:

**Strengths:**
- Positions government as steward of "data commons" for public benefit
- Emphasizes privacy-preserving technologies
- Frames data openness as enabling "citizens, businesses, and oversight bodies to understand how AI systems inform government decisions"

**Weaknesses:**
- Much language centers on government capability: "agents can only be as smart as the information ecosystem they operate within"
- Citizens appear more as data subjects than active participants
- Limited discussion of citizen data sovereignty

**Key quote showing tension:** "Government's unique access to specialised domain information... puts them in a position to enable the creation of training resources that private companies cannot easily replicate"

This positions government as data provider to AI systems rather than trustee of citizen data.

### Accountability & Democratic Oversight

**Assessment: Partial**

Some oversight mechanisms mentioned:
- "For non-public data, a unified data portal can provide a holistic overview... including internal data catalogues and use-case specific access rules"
- Privacy-preserving technologies as safeguard

However, limited discussion of:
- How citizens consent to data use
- Democratic processes for setting data governance rules
- Mechanisms for challenging data use decisions

### Outside-In Pressures

**Assessment: Partially Addressed**

The chapter acknowledges external actors:
- "Open by default" enables external innovation
- Privacy-preserving tech allows research "without exposing individual records"
- Ecosystem-level collaboration across sectors

But it's largely **inside-out** framing — government decides what to open, how to share, etc. Less attention to external pressures like:
- Civil society demanding data access
- Citizen rights to their own data
- Adversarial actors misusing open data

One question does raise this: "How should governments balance data openness with security concerns when agentic systems can analyse and potentially exploit publicly available information?"

### Multi-Vector Theory of Change

**Assessment: Partially Acknowledged**

Primary vector is government action:
- Government builds data infrastructure
- Government sets sharing rules
- Government enables external use

Some acknowledgment of other vectors:
- "academia, private companies, and civic technologists to build upon government AI work"
- Ecosystem collaboration

But civil society as a *driver* of change (demanding data rights, forcing transparency) is underemphasized.

### Gap Assessment

**🟡 YELLOW**

The layer is compatible with public interest framing but needs reframing/additions:
- Strong on operational data management
- Moderate on privacy protections
- Weak on citizen data sovereignty and democratic data governance

### Specific Recommendations

1. **Citizen data sovereignty section:** Add discussion of citizens' rights over their own data — not just government protecting data *about* them, but citizens *controlling* data about themselves

2. **Democratic data governance:** How do parliaments, not just technical experts, set data policies? What's the role of public deliberation?

3. **Data as public trust:** Reframe from "data as infrastructure" (government operational need) to "data as public trust" (government holds on behalf of citizens)

4. **Consent and legitimacy:** More explicit treatment of how citizens consent to data collection and use in an agentic world

5. **Civil society access:** Strengthen framing around civil society's right to access government data for oversight purposes

6. **AGI data implications:** As models approach AGI, data aggregation becomes even more powerful/dangerous — what additional safeguards are needed?

## Layer 9: Tech Stack

### Current Framing

The chapter is explicitly technical, covering five infrastructure layers:
1. Multimodal User Interfaces
2. Application Infrastructure (including LLMs)
3. Orchestration and Interoperability
4. Agentic Digital Public Infrastructure
5. Compute Infrastructure

It emphasizes there is "no canonical 'agentic tech stack'" and focuses on capabilities needed rather than prescriptive solutions.

### Public Interest Alignment

**Assessment: Weak**

This is primarily an **operational capability chapter** with limited public interest framing:

- Framed around "what government needs to build"
- Citizens appear mainly as users of interfaces
- Little discussion of how technical choices serve public purposes

**Example of operational framing:**
> "AI agents excel at interpreting natural language requests and coordinating complex, multi-step responses. Citizens should be able to say 'I am starting a business'..."

Citizens are positioned as service recipients, not rights-holders or democratic principals.

**One stronger moment:**
> "Audit and compliance infrastructure maintaining comprehensive, tamper-evident logs of all agent actions... with real-time monitoring capabilities for regulatory compliance and democratic accountability"

But this is an exception — most of the chapter doesn't connect technical choices to public values.

### Accountability & Democratic Oversight

**Assessment: Partial**

Some mechanisms mentioned:
- "Explainability and audit infrastructure capturing the reasoning process behind LLM outputs"
- "Democratic accountability" mentioned in passing
- Testing and staging infrastructure includes "policy compliance validation"

But oversight is framed technically, not democratically:
- Who decides which technical choices to make?
- How do parliaments oversee infrastructure decisions?
- What role do citizens have in technical governance?

### Outside-In Pressures

**Assessment: Not Addressed**

The chapter is entirely **inside-out** — what government should build. No discussion of:
- External technical pressures (private AI systems that government must interface with)
- Civil society technical capacity
- Adversarial technical actors (covered in Layer 10 instead)
- Citizen expectations shaping technical choices

### Multi-Vector Theory of Change

**Assessment: Government-Only**

The chapter assumes government builds this infrastructure, with vendor participation through procurement. No discussion of:
- Open source communities
- Civil society technical contributions
- Permissionless innovation that government must accommodate

### Gap Assessment

**🟡 YELLOW**

The layer needs reframing to connect technical choices to public interest:
- Currently reads as an IT strategy document
- Needs explicit public interest justification for technical decisions
- Could benefit from "design for democracy" principles

### Specific Recommendations

1. **"Public Interest by Design" section:** Add explicit discussion of how each technical layer serves public values — sovereignty, accountability, accessibility, equity

2. **Democratic technical governance:** Who decides technical architecture? How do elected officials oversee these choices? What role for public deliberation on technical direction?

3. **Open source and public goods:** Strengthen discussion of open-source as default for public infrastructure — both for sovereignty and citizen trust

4. **Accessibility as non-negotiable:** Current accessibility section is good but buried. Elevate it as a core public interest requirement

5. **Outside-in integration:** Add section on how government tech must interface with citizen agents, civil society tools, and the broader AI ecosystem

6. **AGI-readiness:** As models become more capable, architecture choices matter more. What technical choices preserve human oversight as systems grow more autonomous?

7. **Vendor independence framing:** Reframe vendor independence not just as cost/flexibility concern but as sovereignty and democratic control issue

## Layer 10: Cyber Security and Resilience

### Current Framing

The chapter argues for a paradigm shift from "fortress-based cybersecurity" to a "public health approach" that:
- Assumes compromise is endemic
- Builds adaptive immune responses
- Creates strategic boundaries between risk zones

It covers new agentic attack vectors (decision manipulation, agent impersonation, supply chain infiltration, cross-agent privilege escalation) and proposes "security as ecosystem health."

### Public Interest Alignment

**Assessment: Moderate**

Security is inherently about protecting the public, but the chapter frames this primarily operationally:

**Strengths:**
- Protection of citizen data implicit throughout
- Government capability serves citizen protection
- Scenario analysis shows how attacks would harm citizens (fraudulent benefits)

**Weaknesses:**
- Citizens largely invisible — they're protected *by* security, not participants in it
- Limited discussion of security vs. civil liberties tradeoffs
- No mention of surveillance overreach as a security concern

**Key quote showing both strength and gap:**
> "Security protocols requiring legal authorisation generate complete audit trails while maintaining due process requirements, ensuring emergency measures operate within legal boundaries while effectively protecting government operations and preserving citizen rights."

Rights are mentioned, but as constraints rather than purposes.

### Accountability & Democratic Oversight

**Assessment: Partial**

Some mechanisms present:
- "Legal Oversight Preserves Rights During Security Response"
- Complete audit trails
- "automated systems ensuring constitutional constraints are maintained"

But significant gaps:
- Who sets security policies? (Technical vs. democratic)
- How do citizens know what security measures apply to them?
- Parliamentary oversight of security architecture?

The chapter addresses operational accountability (incident response, recovery) but not democratic accountability (who authorized this security posture?).

### Outside-In Pressures

**Assessment: Addressed**

Strong on adversarial outside-in pressures:
- "Bad actors use agents to automate their operations"
- Supply chain attacks
- Foreign adversaries

But less attention to:
- Civil society security researchers
- Citizen security concerns driving policy
- International norms/treaties for agent security

### Multi-Vector Theory of Change

**Assessment: Partially Acknowledged**

Primary vector is government building security capability. Some acknowledgment of:
- Private sector security coordination
- "Without shared rules, this creates chaos"

But civil society as security contributor (bug bounties, independent audits, security research) is underemphasized.

### Gap Assessment

**🟡 YELLOW**

The layer handles operational security well but needs:
- More explicit public interest justification
- Security vs. liberty tradeoff discussion
- Democratic governance of security posture
- Citizen visibility into security measures

### Specific Recommendations

1. **Security vs. surveillance section:** Explicitly address the risk that security measures become surveillance. Quote from earlier layer: "What safeguards prevent compliance systems from drifting into general-purpose surveillance?"

2. **Democratic security governance:** How do parliaments set security policy? What's the role of public debate about security/privacy tradeoffs?

3. **Citizen transparency:** What do citizens know about how their interactions are secured? Can they verify agent authenticity?

4. **Civil society security role:** Bug bounties, independent security research, watchdog access to test government systems

5. **International dimension:** Treaties and norms for agentic security, especially given cross-border systems

6. **AGI security implications:** As systems become more capable, attack and defense both become more powerful. What principles guide this escalation?

## Layer 11: Public Finance and Buying Agents

### Current Framing

The chapter argues that AI procurement represents a "fiscal transformation" — moving from buying products to buying intelligence, from capital expenditure to operational, from fixed costs to variable outcome-based pricing.

Two parts:
1. Procuring AI Agents as Services (adaptive contracting, outcome-based pricing)
2. Public Finance Impact (doing more with less, workforce transition)

**Key quote:** "Governments are shifting from buying hardware and fixed services to procuring intelligence as a service."

### Public Interest Alignment

**Assessment: Weak to Moderate**

The chapter is heavily focused on government efficiency and fiscal management:

**Efficiency framing dominates:**
- "doing significantly more with substantially less"
- "up to 35% cost reductions"
- "maximising taxpayer value"

**Some public interest elements:**
- "taxpayer value" mentioned
- Discussion of workforce transition acknowledges social impact
- Questions about accountability when vendors profit from government efficiency

**Gap:** Limited discussion of:
- How procurement choices serve public purposes beyond efficiency
- Citizen input into what government buys
- Public value beyond cost savings

### Accountability & Democratic Oversight

**Assessment: Partial**

Procurement accountability mentioned:
- "What governance mechanisms prevent digital public infrastructure from becoming rigid constraints?"
- Liability questions when AI makes autonomous decisions
- Audit requirements

But procurement decisions themselves aren't framed democratically:
- Parliament's role in procurement policy?
- Public transparency about what's being bought?
- Citizen input into public purchasing priorities?

### Outside-In Pressures

**Assessment: Partially Addressed**

Markets as external pressure acknowledged:
- Government must adapt to "changing and increasingly automated market place"
- Competition among vendors

But civil society pressures on procurement largely absent:
- Advocacy for ethical AI procurement
- Demands for transparency in AI contracts
- Public interest criteria in procurement

### Multi-Vector Theory of Change

**Assessment: Government-Only (with vendors)**

Primary vector is government procurement from vendors. Some acknowledgment of:
- "fostering a vibrant AI business ecosystem"
- Build vs. buy decisions

But no discussion of:
- Open source alternatives to commercial procurement
- Civil society as AI provider (academic models, nonprofit tools)
- Citizen-driven AI development

### Gap Assessment

**🟡 YELLOW**

The layer needs significant reframing:
- Currently reads as fiscal/procurement strategy
- Limited public interest justification
- Missing democratic accountability in purchasing decisions

### Specific Recommendations

1. **Public value procurement:** Reframe from "taxpayer value" (cost savings) to "public value" (outcomes that serve the public interest). What capabilities should government buy to serve democratic purposes?

2. **Democratic procurement governance:** How do parliaments oversee AI procurement? What transparency requirements for AI contracts?

3. **Ethical procurement criteria:** Beyond efficiency — fairness, sustainability, sovereignty, open source preference

4. **Civil society alternatives:** Not all AI must come from vendors. Academic models, open source, nonprofit tools as alternatives

5. **Workforce transition as public interest:** Reframe workforce impacts not just as "fiscal challenge" but as social contract issue requiring democratic deliberation

6. **Vendor accountability for public outcomes:** Beyond contract compliance — vendors should be accountable for public interest outcomes, not just efficiency metrics

7. **Public transparency:** What AI is government buying? Citizens should know.

## Layer 12: People, Culture, and Leadership

### Current Framing

The chapter argues that Weberian bureaucracy (hierarchy, rules, process compliance) cannot lead agentic transformation. It proposes:
- Leadership that defines outcomes rather than procedures
- Cultures that reward experimentation
- Workforces able to collaborate with AI agents

Four forces reshape public administration:
1. Great Shift toward complex tasks (automation eliminates routine work)
2. Skills Convergence (democratization of technical capabilities)
3. Talent Competition (government competes for AI-native leaders)
4. Cultural Shift (adaptation and collaboration vs. hierarchy)

### Public Interest Alignment

**Assessment: Moderate**

The chapter has elements of both efficiency framing and public purpose:

**Efficiency framing:**
- "doing significantly more with substantially less"
- Workforce as resource to be optimized
- Talent competition as operational challenge

**Public purpose elements:**
- Preserving "best elements of bureaucratic governance — merit-based hiring, institutional continuity, accountability"
- Leaders with "public purpose"
- "Agentic AI enables public servants to focus on the uniquely human qualities of creativity, empathy, and sound judgment"

**Quote bridging both:** "What governments should achieve in an AI-enabled world" — though this is framed as leadership question, not democratic question.

### Accountability & Democratic Oversight

**Assessment: Partial**

Some accountability elements:
- "The shift from process to outcome focus cannot undermine political accountability — it must strengthen it"
- "Elected officials and public servants need clearer visibility into what the government is actually achieving"
- "Oversight evolves from micromanaging procedures to setting strategic direction and evaluating results"

But gaps:
- How do citizens hold outcome-focused government accountable?
- Democratic deliberation about what outcomes to pursue?
- Public input into workforce transformation decisions?

### Outside-In Pressures

**Assessment: Partially Addressed**

External pressures mentioned:
- "widening cultural gap between government and the digital environments in which citizens and businesses operate"
- Talent competition with private sector
- Citizen expectations

But largely framed as pressures government must respond to operationally, not as legitimate democratic demands.

### Multi-Vector Theory of Change

**Assessment: Government-Only**

The chapter focuses entirely on how government transforms itself:
- Leadership development
- Culture change
- Workforce reskilling

No discussion of:
- Civil society pressure for government transformation
- Citizen expectations driving change
- External accountability mechanisms

### Gap Assessment

**🟡 YELLOW**

The layer is compatible with public interest framing but needs additions:
- Strong on operational transformation
- Moderate on preserving accountability
- Weak on democratic governance of transformation itself

### Specific Recommendations

1. **Democratic mandate for transformation:** Who decides the direction of government transformation? Not just leaders — parliament, citizens. The "machine-readable mission" should be democratically determined.

2. **Public servants as public interest trustees:** Reframe workforce not just as capability to develop but as trustees of public interest. Their judgment matters precisely because they serve democratic purposes.

3. **Citizen input into outcome definition:** "Outcome-driven organisations" need democratically-determined outcomes. How do citizens participate in defining what government should achieve?

4. **Workforce transition as social contract:** The "workforce transition economics" section treats this as fiscal issue. But there's a social contract with public servants that requires democratic deliberation, not just management.

5. **Cultural accountability:** The shift from "rule-following" to "outcome-orientation" risks losing accountability. How do we ensure the new culture remains democratically accountable?

6. **Leadership as democratic service:** Reframe AI-native leadership as service to democratic purposes, not just technical competence.

7. **Public deliberation on AI in government:** Citizens should have a voice in how their government uses AI — not just as service recipients but as democratic principals.

## Cross-Layer Synthesis

### Strongest on Public Interest (GREEN)
- **Layer 7 (Agent Governance):** Explicitly centers citizen rights, plural oversight, contestation mechanisms

### Compatible but Need Reframing (YELLOW)
- **Layer 8 (Data and Privacy):** Strong on privacy protection, weak on citizen data sovereignty
- **Layer 9 (Tech Stack):** Operational focus, needs public interest justification
- **Layer 10 (Cyber Security):** Good on protection, needs security/liberty balance and democratic governance
- **Layer 11 (Public Finance):** Efficiency-focused, needs public value framing
- **Layer 12 (People, Culture):** Strong on transformation, needs democratic governance of transformation

### Common Gaps Across Layers

1. **Citizens as principals, not just beneficiaries:** Most layers position citizens as service recipients rather than democratic principals who ultimately authorize government action.

2. **Democratic deliberation:** Limited discussion of how parliaments, public debate, and citizen input shape technical, fiscal, and organizational decisions.

3. **Civil society as active participant:** Layers underemphasize civil society as driver of change, source of oversight, and legitimate voice in governance.

4. **AGI-specific considerations:** Most layers assume narrow AI agents. As systems approach AGI, the stakes intensify across all dimensions — accountability, data, security, procurement, workforce.

5. **International democratic governance:** Cross-border AI systems require international governance frameworks that are themselves democratically legitimate.

### Recommended Cross-Cutting Additions

1. **"Democratic Governance of the Agentic State" section** in each layer addressing:
   - Parliament's role
   - Public deliberation mechanisms
   - Citizen rights and input channels

2. **Civil society throughout:** Each layer should address civil society not just as beneficiary but as participant in governance, oversight, and innovation.

3. **AGI readiness:** Each layer should address how its mechanisms scale as AI systems become more capable and approach AGI.

4. **International dimension:** Each layer should address cross-border governance and international democratic cooperation.

---

## Conclusion

The Enablement Layers 7-12 range from strongly public-interest-aligned (Layer 7) to primarily operational (Layers 9, 11). None have fundamental tensions with public interest framing — they're all compatible — but most need additions or reframing to fully answer the core question:

> "In an era of many AGIs, how do we ensure they serve the public interest?"

The most consistent gap is the positioning of citizens as service recipients rather than democratic principals. The paper would be strengthened by consistently framing government AI capability as serving democratically-determined public purposes, with mechanisms for citizen input, parliamentary oversight, and civil society participation throughout.

Layer 7 provides a model for how this can work — it explicitly creates plural oversight mechanisms where civil society and citizens are active participants in governance, not just beneficiaries of protection. Applying similar principles across the other layers would significantly strengthen the public interest framing of the entire paper.
